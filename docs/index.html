Which content pages should a reviewer check first?

A behavior-based priority queue for content review, built and honestly validated on real FlyRank production search data — with an explicit list of what it can't claim.

Research Paper — Applied Search Intelligence

Content Archetype Clustering: Grouping Pages by Observed Behavior to Prioritize a Review Queue

Yumna Kashif · FlyRank ML Internship, Applied Search Intelligence track · repo ↗

Abstract

When a site has far more pages than a review team can check by hand, which page should a human look at first? This paper groups 30,000 real content pages by five observed signals — impressions, click-through rate, ranking position, engagement, and age — using KMeans clustering, and compares the result to a transparent hand-written rule. Clustering was validated honestly by holding out entire clients (not just rows), which dropped the separation score from an inflated 0.376 to a real 0.305 — still far ahead of the rule-based baseline's 0.0013. The resulting archetypes turn into a ranked action playbook — refresh, boost, prune, or monitor — with reason codes, a mandatory human-review flag on 23% of pages, and an explicit no-go list for what should never be automated. This is decision-support for a content team, not a causal or production system: every claim below is stated at the strength its evidence actually carries.

01

Introduction & Problem

FlyRank publishes content into client websites at a scale no team can review page-by-page: the production catalog behind this project holds 519,606 content items across 104 clients. Pages that rank well quietly decay over time — rankings slip, clicks drop — and by the time a team notices, the page has often been losing traffic for weeks.

FlyRank's product already flags pages today with hand-written rules (a health score, quick-win tags). Those rules work and run in production, but they test one condition at a time. A page can have high impressions and a broken click-through rate, or low impressions and strong engagement — real pages fail on combinations of signals, not one threshold. This paper asks whether grouping pages by several observed signals at once, instead of one rule at a time, gives a reviewer a sharper starting point.

The decision this supports: which pages a content strategist opens first this week. The cost of getting it wrong: a page wrongly read as healthy keeps losing traffic unnoticed; a page wrongly flagged burns editorial hours it didn't need. Every output here is built to be checked by a person before anything happens to a page — never to act on its own.

03

Methodology

Features (5, all trailing-90-day observed metrics): impressions, click-through rate, average ranking position, engagement rate, and content age — standardized before clustering so no single scale dominates.

Baseline, built first: a transparent rule with no fitted weights. Two candidate signals were tested against the data before either went into it:

  • Staleness → decline? Mixed. Decline rate rises from 51.1% to 61.1% across most freshness buckets, then drops to 47.1% in the oldest bucket (n=174) — the opposite of the assumption. Dropped.
  • Position → CTR? Confirmed. Median CTR falls in lock-step with ranking position across every well-sampled bucket (top_3 0.20% → deep 0.00%). Kept as the rule's core signal.

The baseline score multiplies a visibility percentile by a CTR-gap percentile, restricted to eligible pages, sorted into three fixed-threshold buckets.

Model: KMeans, k chosen by silhouette search over k=2..7 (k=7 won). Chosen over a supervised model because this lane has no label to predict, and its output — cluster membership — is easy to explain to a non-technical reviewer.

Validation: the first fit was leaky — centroids fit and scored on the same 30,000 rows. The honest fix: split all 55 client IDs (not rows) 80/20, fit centroids on the 80% train-client rows only, then score strictly on the 20% held-out clients the model never touched — testing whether archetypes generalize to a brand-new client.

Leakage checks run: (1) k-selection leakage — re-ran the k search on train-clients-only data; k=7 won there too, so the winning k doesn't depend on seeing the held-out clients. (2) Feature leakage — confirmed IDs and the label source never enter the feature list. (3) A separate label-leakage trap (data-contract exercise) showed a feature built from the same window as a toy label pushed accuracy from a believable 0.591 to a suspicious 1.000 — that pattern is excluded from every model in this paper.

Cluster archetypes

Profiled on all 30,000 rows: one small cluster of very high-traffic top performers (avg. 112k impressions/90d); two large clusters of steady, older content differing mainly by age; one low-CTR, poorly-ranked cluster (avg. position 46); and a few small, unusual clusters — one near-zero-impression/high-CTR group (likely low-volume, highly targeted queries), one unusually high-engagement group. About 2.6% of all points are poorly matched to their assigned cluster, concentrated in one borderline-engagement group sitting between two clearer archetypes.

05

Limitations & Honest Framing

  • Cross-sectional, not causal. No page was actually refreshed and re-measured — nothing here says acting on a recommendation will improve traffic, only that certain pages look worth reviewing first.
  • Not "predicting Google." Clusters group behavior on 5 measured signals, not the ranking algorithm itself, and this is not semantic clustering — no page text was used.
  • Small held-out sample for the decline-rate finding. The 14.6%–61.9% range is measured on 7 held-out clients — real, but too small to say it generalizes to the full 104-client base.
  • Starter sample, not the full catalog. The main pipeline ran on 30,000 of 519,606 content items; cluster shapes haven't been re-validated at full scale.
  • Baseline wasn't re-tested under the same honest split — only KMeans was re-scored on held-out clients; the rule buckets were evaluated once, on all data.
  • Single snapshot, not a real time series. "Decay" here means declining in one 30-vs-prior-30-day window, observed once.
  • Playbook thresholds are simple, un-tuned cutoffs — a starting shape for the queue, not yet validated against real editor decisions or downstream outcomes.

Ranked Recommendations

Every page in the sample gets one of four actions, driven only by real, observed signals — position tier, CTR gap, decline label, impressions, and cluster fit. No invented columns.

PriorityActionTriggern

23.4% of the queue is flagged for mandatory human sign-off — every PRUNE candidate, every top-10%-visibility page, every score in the uncertain band, and every weak cluster fit.

Never automated: URL deletion or redirection without human verification; core brand/legal pages (hard-excluded); site navigation or URL-structure changes based solely on this output.
